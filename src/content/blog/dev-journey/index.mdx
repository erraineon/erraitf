I was fascinated by computer bugs ever since I was eight. My first encounter was [Glitch City](https://bulbapedia.bulbagarden.net/wiki/Glitch_City) in Pokemon Red. Saving and reloading the game while in the Safari Zone would lead to a corrupted map filled with random tiles and no way out. 

Back then, these urban legends would spread via word of mouth (see [Mew Under The Truck](https://gaming-urban-legends.fandom.com/wiki/Mew_Under_the_Truck)). So I thought that Glitch City was a deliberate secret place hidden by the game's developers, and couldn't help but wonder: what else laid beyond those broken lands? My imagination ran wild.

Later that summer, another kid told me about MissingNo. I'm still not sure how he knew the exact steps to perform the glitch, given that it only applied to English copies of the game. I'm also not sure why I happened to own one, given I lived in Italy. Anyway, A few weeks later, having returned from summer camp and regained access to my GameBoy, I gave it a try.

<FloatingFigure image={m} alt="MissingNo encounter">
  MissingNo, or more precisely, ['M (00)](https://bulbapedia.bulbagarden.net/wiki/%27M_(00)).
</FloatingFigure>

The first time MissingNo's jagged silhouette appeared on screen, I felt an adrenaline rush. Seeing level 127 Pokemon in the wild, when the whole time the game enforced the "Level 100 is the limit" framework, felt forbidden and exciting. It felt like meeting a ghost in the machine. Exploring further was scary, but I couldn't resist.

Not long after, I acquired my first Gameshark. Just eight digits (`0115D8CF`) would trigger a Mew encounter, an otherwise unobtainable Pokemon. I had no idea of why or how, and was only armed with a game console, my curiosity, and a lot of free time.

<FloatingFigure image={xploder} alt="Blaze Xploder">
  The Blaze Xploder was a cartridge acting as a proxy between console and game, and allowing editing and scanning memory values.
</FloatingFigure>

When I eventually upgraded to a GameBoy Advance, Pokemon Ruby, and a GBA-compatible cheat device, I started bringing the setup with me to summer camp and applying cheats to other kids' cartridges. 

Later, I learned that freezing certain memory values was not the same editing the code that wrote them were very different disciplines.

I memorized enough Assembly to write manual code caves in OllyDbg. I banged my head trying to unpack Themida manually, and somehow succeeded. I rode the highs of writing new hacks for several MMOs, and decompiling Undertale's code and assets long before anyone else did.

Almost 30 years later, I realize that it was never about learning how to code. Programming had always been a tool to achieve whichever goal I was after. It was always about my curiosity, and the intoxicating dance around the boundary of the seen and the concealed, the known and the undefined.

I still do game and software reverse engineering -- Animal Well and Noita were two of my most recent challenges. Most of the time I'm not working, however, I channel my coding urges towards creating something new, primarily videogames, rather than dissecting something that's already out there.

I would thank the kid who told me about Glitch City many summers ago, but I think my lead into programming would've happened even if he hadn't. I was always curious, and I always loved videogames. The two locks share a common key.
