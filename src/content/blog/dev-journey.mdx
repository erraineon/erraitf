I was fascinated by computer bugs ever since I was eight. My first encounter was [Glitch City](https://bulbapedia.bulbagarden.net/wiki/Glitch_City) in Pokemon Red. Why would a specific, almost ritualistic set of instructions lead you to a place like that? And more interestingly: what else was hidden beyond those broken lands?

Not long after, I acquired my first Gameshark. Just eight digits (`0115D8CF`) would trigger a Mew encounter, an otherwise unobtainable Pokemon. I had no idea of why or how, and was only armed with a game console, my curiosity, and a lot of free time.

So I noticed that changing two digits in the code above would let you encounter a completely different Pokemon. Later, I learned that freezing certain memory values was not the same editing the code that wrote them were very different disciplines. I learned enough Assembly to write manual code caves in OllyDbg. I banged my head trying to unpack Themida manually, and somehow succeeded. I rode the highs of writing new hacks for several MMOs, and decompiling Undertale's code and assets long before anyone else did.

Almost 30 years later, I realize that it was never about learning how to code. Programming had always been a tool to achieve whichever goal I was after. It was always about my curiosity, and the intoxicating dance around the boundary of the seen and the concealed, the known and the undefined.

I still do game and software reverse engineering -- Animal Well and Noita were two of my most recent challenges. Most of the time I'm not working, however, I channel my coding urges towards creating something new, primarily videogames, rather than dissecting something that's already out there.

I would thank the kid who told me about Glitch City many summers ago, but I think my lead into programming would've happened even if he hadn't. I was always curious, and I always loved videogames. The two locks share a common key.
